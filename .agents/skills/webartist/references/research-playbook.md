# Research playbook: brief → references → moodboard

The point of researching before designing is to escape the *average*. Without
references, you regress to the mean of everything you've seen — which is exactly
the "AI slop" look. Real references pull the design toward something specific
and current.

## Turn the brief into search queries

From the interview answers, build queries along several axes so you don't get
four versions of the same thing:

1. **The subject's real-world domain.** Search the world the product lives in,
   not just "websites" — its materials, packaging, signage, editorial, tools, and
   the vocabulary practitioners use. Borrow textures and language from the actual
   subject in the brief, not from the category's web stereotype.
2. **The category, dated to now.** "best [category] websites 2026",
   "[category] landing page design trends" — the year filters out stale
   patterns from older training data.
3. **The emotional tone.** Combine the `tone` adjectives from the brief with the
   `[category]` — e.g. "minimal editorial [category]", "playful bold [category]
   site", "premium restrained [category] UI" — searching the adjectives the user
   chose, not a stereotype.
4. **Direct competitors / adjacents.** What do the user's competitors do — so
   you can deliberately *not* look like them.
5. **The user's own references.** Anything they named as loved/hated in the
   interview — look at it directly.

## Where to look

Default to **validated, award-winning, community-praised work** — sites that
cleared a quality bar are safer reference templates than the average search
result. Study their structure and craft (composition, pacing, finish) as
templates to synthesize from, never to clone.

Design galleries and inspiration sources worth searching/fetching:

- **Awwwards** — prioritise Site of the Day / Site of the Month and Honorable
  Mentions; these are the award-validated templates to study first.
- **Godly** (godly.website), **Land-book**, **SiteInspire**, **Httpster**,
  **Minimal Gallery** — curated web design with community/editorial vetting.
- **Mobbin** — real app/product UI flows.
- **Dribbble** / **Behance** — concepts (treat as ideas, not as shippable; many
  Dribbble shots ignore usability).
- **Typewolf**, **Fonts In Use** — typography pairings in the wild.
- The subject's **real-world artifacts** — packaging, signage, editorial, art
  from that domain — often the richest, least generic source.

Use `WebSearch` to find candidates, then `WebFetch` on the promising ones to
look closer at what they actually do.

## Build a moodboard (not a copy list)

Favour award-winning or community-praised references over generic results. For
each of **2–4 distinct references**, capture:

- **URL / source**
- **The one thing worth borrowing** — be specific: "the way the display type
  overlaps the image edge", "the muted palette with a single hot accent", "the
  slow staggered reveal on scroll". A principle, not a screenshot to trace.
- **What you will NOT take** — so you stay aware you're synthesizing, not
  cloning. You are extracting *ideas*; the execution must be original and yours.

Aim for references with genuinely different points of view. If all four
references look alike, your design will too — search a wider net.

## Research components, not just the hero

The same "look at real examples" discipline applies below the page level. Before
building a contact form, a footer, a pricing table, a cookie banner, or a
navigation, look at how real sites in the space actually build that element —
because real sites include the unglamorous-but-expected parts that a from-memory
draft drops: privacy-consent checkboxes, cookie banners, multi-column footers
with legal links and socials, newsletter opt-ins, "reason for contact" selects,
expectation-setting copy ("we reply within 2 days"). If your component is missing
things every real version has, you researched the look but not the substance.

## Synthesize, then report

- Combine borrowed *principles* from multiple references into something new —
  the synthesis is the design, not any single source.
- Briefly show the user the references and the direction you're drawing from, so
  they can react before you commit to building. A 3-line summary per reference
  is enough.

## If there's no web access

Say so plainly. Then lean on:
- The subject's own world and vocabulary (still your richest non-generic source).
- The anti-slop checklist to actively avoid defaults.
- The user's named references from the interview.
Don't invent fake "trends" — be honest that you're working without live research.
